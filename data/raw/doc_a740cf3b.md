## Abstract
Limiting the global average temperature rise to 1.5 °C requires an unprecedented reduction in fossil fuel use, along with large-scale deployment of CO2 capture and storage. To track the fossil fuel industry and companies against 1.5 °C-consistent pathways, we propose a new methodology that complements existing methodologies in four main ways: (1) it uses publicly available data; (2) focuses on absolute fossil fuel production (as a proxy for embedded emissions) rather than carbon intensities associated with their use; (3) includes coal that is commonly excluded; and (4) is applicable regardless of whether the company has set a target. By applying this method, we evaluated the 142 largest producers of coal, oil and gas against three 1.5 °C IPCC Shared Socio-economic Pathways (SSP1-1.9, SSP2-1.9 and SSP5-1.9) from 2014 and the International Energy Agency’s Net Zero Emissions pathway from 2020. Between 2014 and 2020, 64%, 63% and 70% of coal, oil and gas companies, respectively, produced more than their production budgets under the IPCC’s middle-of-the-road (SSP2-1.9) Paris Agreement-compliant scenario. In addition, if the 142 companies we examined continued their average growth rate trends from 2010 to 2018, they would produce up to 68%, 42% and 53% more than their cumulative production budgets for coal, oil and gas, respectively, by 2050. By providing such simple metrics, based on publicly available data, our method offers stakeholders a way of easily tracking and comparing the performance of different fossil fuel producers against climate goals.

## Main
Meeting the climate goals negotiated in the 2015 Paris Agreement requires the rapid reduction of the use of fossil fuels and implies that substantial amounts of fossil fuel reserves will remain unburnt[1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR1 "McGlade, C. & Ekins, P. The geographical distribution of fossil fuels unused when limiting global warming to 2 °C. Nature 517, 187–190 \(2015\)."),[2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR2 "Welsby, D., Price, J., Pye, S. & Ekins, P. Unextractable fossil fuels in a 1.5 °C world. Nature 597, 230–234 \(2021\)."),[3](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR3 "Trout, K. et al. Existing fossil fuel extraction would warm the world beyond 1.5 °C. Environ. Res. Lett. 17, 064010 \(2022\)."),[4](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR4 "Kühne, K., Bartsch, N., Tate, R. D., Higson, J. & Habet, A. “Carbon Bombs” – mapping key fossil fuel projects. Energy Policy 166, 112950 \(2022\)."). The prolonged use of coal poses a particular threat to meeting climate goals, and more than 40 countries have committed to end all investment in new coal domestically and internationally following Conference of Parties (COP) 26 (ref. [5](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR5 "Global Coal to Clean Power Transition Statement \(UN Climate Change Conference, 2021\); 
                  https://ukcop26.org/global-coal-to-clean-power-transition-statement/
                  
                ")). Many countries, however, including some that are among the largest emitters, have not committed to phasing out the production or use of coal, or other fossil fuels, with the current commitments post-COP 26 expected to still lead to around 2.6 °C warming (with a range of 2 °C to 3.7 °C)[6](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR6 "Hausfather, Z. & Moore, F. Net-zero commitments could limit warming to below 2 °C. Nat. Clim. Change 604, 247–248 \(2022\).").
While increasing national ambition towards decarbonization is important, it is increasingly recognized that companies will be a critical determinant of whether climate goals are achieved[7](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR7 "Wright, C. & Nyberg, D. An inconvenient truth: how organizations translate climate change into business as usual. Acad. Manage. J. 60, 1633–1661 \(2017\)."),[8](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR8 "Kuramochi, T. et al. Beyond national climate action: the impact of region, city, and business commitments on global greenhouse gas emissions. Clim. Policy 20, 275–291 \(2020\)."). Some investors and asset managers have elected to exclude fossil fuel companies from their portfolios[9](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR9 "Rohleder, M., Wilkens, M. & Zink, J. The effects of mutual fund decarbonisation on stock prices and carbon emissions. J. Bank. Financ. 134, 106352 \(2022\).") and/or are pressuring fossil fuel companies to align their activities with the Paris Agreement. Coinciding with such actions, an increasing number of emission reduction targets has been announced by fossil fuel companies[10](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR10 "Dietz, S., Gardiner, D., Jahn, V. & Noels, J. How ambitious are oil and gas companies’ climate goals? Science 374, 405–408 \(2021\)."). However, recent work demonstrates that only one of the major oil and gas companies has targets that are consistent with 1.5 °C-aligned IPCC pathways[10](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR10 "Dietz, S., Gardiner, D., Jahn, V. & Noels, J. How ambitious are oil and gas companies’ climate goals? Science 374, 405–408 \(2021\).").
Aligning the promises and performance of any individual entity’s climate goals requires the global carbon budget, or the 1.5 °C and well-below 2 °C (WB2D) mitigation pathways, to be allocated over time to each entity[11](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR11 "Hadziosmanovic, M. et al. Using cumulative carbon budgets and corporate carbon disclosure to inform ambitious corporate emissions targets and long‐term mitigation pathways. J. Ind. Ecol. 26, 1747–1759 \(2022\)."). This is not a straightforward task. For nations, there has been a 30-yr ongoing process of international negotiations culminating with the National Determined Contributions based loosely around ‘common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities’[12](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR12 "United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change Article 3, section 1 \(United Nations, 1992\); 
                  https://unfccc.int/resource/docs/convkp/conveng.pdf
                  
                "),[13](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR13 "Pauw, P., Mbeva, K. & van Asselt, H. Subtle differentiation of countries’ responsibilities under the Paris Agreement. Palgrave Commun. 5, 86 \(2019\)."). Yet, allocation methodologies are fundamental for tracking progress, assigning responsibilities and examining financial risks of inaction. For companies in particular, this information is crucial for stakeholders to assess investment risks and make informed and climate-safe decisions[14](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR14 "Rekker, S., Ives, M. C., Wade, B., Webb, L. & Greig, C. Measuring corporate Paris Compliance using a strict science-based approach. Nat. Commun. 13, 4441 \(2022\)."). In recent years, several methods have been developed to assess the alignment of companies to the Paris goals, including for specific carbon-intensive sectors such as the fossil fuel sector[15](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR15 "Krabbe, O. et al. Aligning corporate greenhouse-gas emissions targets with climate goals. Nat. Clim. Change 5, 1057–1060 \(2015\)."),[16](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR16 "Guidance on Setting Science-based Targets for Oil, Gas and Integrated Energy companies \(Science Based Targets, 2021\); 
                  https://sciencebasedtargets.org/resources/legacy/2020/08/OG-Guidance.pdf
                  
                "),[17](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR17 "Rekker, S., O’Brien, K., Humphrey, J. & Pascale, A. Comparing extraction rates of fossil fuel producers against global climate goals. Nat. Clim. Change 8, 489–492 \(2018\)."). A special approach is needed for fossil fuel companies, since it is the use of their product, often by third parties (as so-called scope 3 emissions), that has the most influence on global emissions[17](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR17 "Rekker, S., O’Brien, K., Humphrey, J. & Pascale, A. Comparing extraction rates of fossil fuel producers against global climate goals. Nat. Clim. Change 8, 489–492 \(2018\)."),[18](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR18 "Heede, R. Tracing anthropogenic carbon dioxide and methane emissions to fossil fuel and cement producers 1854–2010. Clim. Change 122, 229–241 \(2014\).").
Several methodologies to track the performance of the fossil fuel industry and its individual constituents against climate goals have been developed so far, but each has key shortcomings we intend to address in this paper. First, current methodologies vary in their complexity, with some requiring comprehensive corporate carbon accounting methods with details of the companies’ processes that are not publicly available or are costly to obtain (for a comprehensive review of the methods see [Supplementary Information](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#MOESM1)). In particular, the Science Based Targets initiative (SBTi) uses an intensity approach for oil and gas companies, requiring granular company data that are not freely available (although companies currently cannot set targets as the methodology is being redesigned)[16](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR16 "Guidance on Setting Science-based Targets for Oil, Gas and Integrated Energy companies \(Science Based Targets, 2021\); 
                  https://sciencebasedtargets.org/resources/legacy/2020/08/OG-Guidance.pdf
                  
                "). Our proposed approach requires less data and only data that are publicly available, promoting transparency and ease of use. Second, methodologies relying on carbon intensity metrics need to be complemented with absolute emission reduction levels to ensure global carbon budgets are not exceeded[10](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR10 "Dietz, S., Gardiner, D., Jahn, V. & Noels, J. How ambitious are oil and gas companies’ climate goals? Science 374, 405–408 \(2021\)."),[15](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR15 "Krabbe, O. et al. Aligning corporate greenhouse-gas emissions targets with climate goals. Nat. Clim. Change 5, 1057–1060 \(2015\)."),[19](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR19 "Absolute Impact 2021: Why Oil and Gas ‘Net Zero’ Ambitions are Not Enough \(Carbon Tracker, 2021\); 
                  https://carbontracker.org/reports/absolute-impact-2021/
                  
                "). A recent Shell legal ruling demonstrates that courts can conclude that intensity targets are not sufficient[20](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR20 "How We Defeated Shell: Milieudefensie et al. v. Royal Dutch Shell PLC – A Peek Behind the Scenes \(Milieudefensie, 2021\)."),[21](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR21 "Cox, R. & Reij, M. Defending the Danger Line: A Manual for Climate Litigators: Using the Law as Climate Action Tool to Achieve the Paris Temperature Goal \(Paulussen Advocaten & Milieudefensie, 2022\)."). We therefore focus on comparing fossil fuel ‘production’ to Paris-compliant fossil fuel demand projections, thus shifting the focus from ‘intensity’ to ‘absolute’ measures. Third, coal is excluded from many current methodologies, whereas our method includes coal. Finally, our approach enables us to evaluate a uniquely large dataset of 74 coal companies representing 56% of global coal production, 67 oil companies representing 75% of global oil production and 70 natural gas companies responsible for 74% of global natural gas production during the period 2010–2020. These companies together have produced 70% of global fossil fuels on a primary energy basis over the same period.
Our analysis is presented in two parts: first, we demonstrate how our method can be used to evaluate the performance of individual companies and develop company-specific pathways consistent with limiting temperature rise to 1.5 °C compared to pre-industrial levels and second, we apply our methodology to the performance of a sample of 142 fossil fuel companies.
## Production as an absolute measure to assess Paris alignment
Almost all decarbonization scenarios that are consistent with limiting warming to WB2D require a rapid future decline in fossil fuel use. Aligning production with these demand constraints informs fossil fuel producers of the unavoidable need to change their business model, complementing the current carbon intensity convergence models for science-based target setting. For a company to align with the Paris goals, the following are required: (1) The underlying decarbonization pathway used should be consistent with ‘WB2D above pre-industrial levels’ and ‘pursuing efforts to limit global temperature rise to 1.5 °C’. (2) The base year from which progress is measured must be consistent with the initial year of the decarbonization pathway. (3) The decarbonization pathway should commence in 2015 or earlier[14](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR14 "Rekker, S., Ives, M. C., Wade, B., Webb, L. & Greig, C. Measuring corporate Paris Compliance using a strict science-based approach. Nat. Commun. 13, 4441 \(2022\)."). If companies use a base year that is not aligned with the commencement of the underlying decarbonization pathway, it is difficult to evaluate their alignment with that particular decarbonization pathway. For example, if a company sets a base year of 2015 but compared itself with a decarbonization pathway commencing in 2010, the company may have been mis-aligned historically without being held accountable. In other words, a peer company that has been compliant with the pathway from the base year onwards would be unfairly disadvantaged.
We use three sample pathways commencing in 2014 to demonstrate our method. The three pathways we use are from different models (all using a 1.9 Representative Concentration Pathway (RCP), consistent with 1.5 °C) and applied to three different Shared Socio-economic Pathways (SSPs): the Asia-Pacific Integrated Modelling/Computable General Equilibrium (AIM/CGE SSP1–1.9, ‘SSP1–1.9’ hereafter), the Model for Energy Supply Strategy Alternatives and their General Environmental Impact-Global Biosphere Management Model (MESSAGE-GLOBIOM SSP2–1.9, ‘SSP2–1.9’ hereafter) and the Global Change Assessment Model (GCAM4 SSP5–1.9, ‘SSP5–1.9’ hereafter). We chose SSP2 given that it is the ‘middle of the road’ scenario, as well as SSP1 (‘the green road’) and SSP5 (the ‘fossil-fuelled development’) scenarios for comparison (see [Methods](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#Sec4) for further explanation). The pathways model different levels of cumulative fossil fuel production levels by 2050 and require varying levels of carbon capture and storage (CCS) coupled with fossil fuel use and other forms of carbon removal, such as bioenergy with CCS (BECCS) and direct air capture with CCS (DACCS). In 2050, total CCS (including fossil CCS, BECCS and DACCS) required under the scenarios is 6.3, 8.6 and 32.4 GtCO2 yr−1 for SSP1, 2 and 5, respectively (Table [1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#Tab1)). In addition, in the year 2050 under SSP1–1.9, SSP2–1.9 and SSP5–1.9, 68%, 38% and 187%, respectively, of the CO2 emissions of fossil fuel and industry (after deducting CCS) should be offset by land use improvements and carbon dioxide removal. In 2050, SSP5–1.9 relies on net-negative CO2 emissions from 2050, while SSP1–1.9 and SSP2–1.9 involve net-negative CO2 emissions from 2060. The different characteristics of the three scenarios therefore result in different levels of fossil fuel production budgets. We also model the implications of following the International Energy Agency’s Net Zero Emission (IEA-NZE) pathway, which starts in 2020, for comparison. Similar to the three pathways (SSP1, 2, 5–1.9), IEA-NZE is also consistent with 1.5 °C without a temperature overshoot (with a 50% probability), while also providing a pathway for the energy sector to achieve net-zero CO2 emissions by 2050. It relies on 7.6 GtCO2 yr−1 of total CCS in 2050. The IEA is an organization whose reports are widely cited among fossil fuel producers and governments.

Allocating the global fossil fuel production budget among producers in a way that is consistent with the IPCC pathways can be achieved using various approaches that account for historical production levels, carbon intensities, reserve levels, costs and economic capabilities, geopolitical settings, socio-economic equity principles and many more (see Supplementary Information, Section [2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#MOESM1)). Alternatively, allocating future production budgets according to reserves can create large differences compared with a production method, particularly for state-owned entities that have typically higher reserve-to-production ratios (Extended Data Fig. [1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#Fig4) (refs. [17](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR17 "Rekker, S., O’Brien, K., Humphrey, J. & Pascale, A. Comparing extraction rates of fossil fuel producers against global climate goals. Nat. Clim. Change 8, 489–492 \(2018\)."),[22](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR22 "Heede, R. & Oreskes, N. Potential emissions of CO2 and methane from proved reserves of fossil fuels: an alternative analysis. Glob. Environ. Change 36, 12–20 \(2016\)."))). For demonstration purposes, our method allocates the future production budget using average 2010–2014 production levels for two reasons: (1) due to data availability (widely available compared with other variables such as reserves or costs) and (2) to account for recent production before the base year of 2014 and thus not relying on a single observation in time. Few significant differences are found in the choices for allocating the budget according to historical contributions between 1980–2014 and 2010–2014 (see Extended Data Fig. [1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#Fig4)). By using the average production rate of 2010–2014 as an allocation mechanism, this study has a clear advantage due to the large amount of production data available using a freely available dataset, resulting in the ability to assess 142 fossil fuel companies. In time, it is hoped that increased disclosure by companies will allow scholars and others to explore alternative criteria for distributing the carbon budget. Here we allocate the annual global production budgets under each decarbonization scenario to each company on the basis of their share of global production in 2010–2014 (see Table [2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#Tab2)) and evaluate their performance from 2014 onwards. This means we adopt a grandfathering approach, whereby companies with higher production shares in 2010–2014 get a larger production share going forward, which has implied equity limitations. Collectively, however, it means that if each company stays within its production ‘budget’, global production will stay within the production limits under the decarbonization scenario, provided of course that the required levels of CCS under the decarbonization scenario are actually deployed. Companies would ideally play an active role in ensuring that these levels of future CCS can be met and be required to verify that the CCS requirements associated with their own production have been and will continue to be met under their chosen trajectory. ## Discussion and conclusions
In this Article, we have proposed a simple and transparent method to evaluate a wide range of fossil fuel companies against climate scenarios. Our methodology complements the current carbon intensity convergence models for science-based target setting in two ways. First, the findings can help fossil fuel companies set science-based targets without conducting carbon accounting. Many fossil fuel companies struggle to provide complete carbon accounting, especially for scope 3 emissions[19](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR19 "Absolute Impact 2021: Why Oil and Gas ‘Net Zero’ Ambitions are Not Enough \(Carbon Tracker, 2021\); 
                  https://carbontracker.org/reports/absolute-impact-2021/
                  
                "). Second, this method complements the previous science-based targets setting method by providing an alternative way to measure performance, that is, production budget or production trajectories for a time series. Our simple method can be applied not only to oil and gas but also to coal producers, supplementing the SBTi standard that only focuses on the oil and gas industry[16](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR16 "Guidance on Setting Science-based Targets for Oil, Gas and Integrated Energy companies \(Science Based Targets, 2021\); 
                  https://sciencebasedtargets.org/resources/legacy/2020/08/OG-Guidance.pdf
                  
                ").
Global stakeholders can use our results to easily assess fossil fuel companies’ performance against NZE and 1.5 °C scenarios without in-house expertise in carbon accounting. This method provides a transparent, albeit approximate, assessment using publicly available production data, thus increasing accessibility and consistency. By focusing solely on production, we avoid carbon accounting methods that increase data requirements and analytical complexity, such as the use of poorly reported scope 3 emissions[19](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR19 "Absolute Impact 2021: Why Oil and Gas ‘Net Zero’ Ambitions are Not Enough \(Carbon Tracker, 2021\); 
                  https://carbontracker.org/reports/absolute-impact-2021/
                  
                "). Thus, for fossil fuel companies where absolute emissions or intensities are not provided or cannot be determined, we provide interested parties with a straightforward methodology. We encourage all fossil fuel companies to use our method to set their production targets in addition to their emissions targets. This will help to improve transparency and consistency for global stakeholders to assess their climate risks. Our method offers a useful contribution for consideration by the Science Based Targets initiative, which is currently revising their SBTi Oil & Gas standard, and to extend the standard to include coal[16](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR16 "Guidance on Setting Science-based Targets for Oil, Gas and Integrated Energy companies \(Science Based Targets, 2021\); 
                  https://sciencebasedtargets.org/resources/legacy/2020/08/OG-Guidance.pdf
                  
                ").
We evaluate the 142 largest producers of coal, oil and gas against three 1.5 °C IPCC SSP–RCP1.9 pathways from 2010. We find that the 142 companies would produce up to 65%, 33% and 55% more than the cumulative production budget of coal, oil and gas, respectively, by 2050 if they continued the trend of the average growth rate for 2010–2018. We clearly highlight the CCS required under the three different IPCC scenarios for each fossil fuel (IEA only has cumulative CCS available, presented in [Source Data](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#Sec11)). Coal production in particular will need to be paired with CCS such that up to 40% of the cumulative fossil fuel emissions have been captured and stored by 2050. In a number of RCP1.9 pathways, even after fossil fuel production has been substantially reduced in the second half of the century, CCS must continue to expand to draw down accumulated carbon dioxide in the atmosphere. Depending on the pathway, CCS rates after mid-century can range from around 5 billion to over 20 billion tonnes of CO2 per annum, some two orders of magnitude more than current global CCS capacity, the feasibility of which is highly uncertain[26](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR26 "Lane, J., Greig, C. & Garnett, A. Uncertain storage prospects create a conundrum for carbon capture and storage ambitions. Nat. Clim. Change 11, 925–936 \(2021\)."). Furthermore, the reliance on negative emissions technologies later in the century to recover from a carbon budget overshoot is a high-risk strategy[27](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR27 "Anderson, K. & Peters, G. The trouble with negative emissions. Science 354, 182–183 \(2016\)."). For companies to claim Paris alignment, we argue that they must be held accountable for the achievement of the levels of mitigation via CCS (including fossil CCS, BECCS and DACCS) projected under their specified pathway. This would include, for example, requirements to produce credible forward deployment plans for CCS, and to report annual CCS development and project capital invested, and actual capacity in service (for example, million tonnes of CO2 being captured and sequestered per year) to give credibility to such pathways.
The approach demonstrated in this Article comes with inevitable caveats and limitations (see [Supplementary Information](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#MOESM1)). Nevertheless, it provides a valuable new approach for evaluating fossil fuel producers’ alignment with 1.5 °C fossil fuel demand trajectories. This is an important tool for a range of stakeholders seeking to assess the performance of fossil fuel companies against Paris-compliant decarbonization pathways, as well as informing fossil fuel producers of the clear and unavoidable need for them to transform their businesses. For stakeholders such as regulators, policymakers, consumers and investors to effectively implement climate-safe decisions, it is important that they have access to granular, robust and accessible information. Specifically, our work can be used to provide guidance for the practical operationalization of a fossil fuel non-proliferation treaty, which is increasingly being called for by nations, Nobel Laureates, academics and health organizations[28](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR28 "Howard, C. et al. Why we need a fossil fuel non-proliferation treaty. Lancet Planet. Health 6, e777–e778 \(2022\).").
Of course, the tool presented here has limitations when it comes to major global shocks and energy supply disruptions. Fossil fuel producers can find themselves being pushed in two opposing directions by such shocks. On the one hand, governments and investors demand that producers respond to the threat posed by climate change and reduce their direct and indirect contributions to global GHG emissions. On the other hand, shocks such as the invasion of Ukraine and the subsequent sanctions imposed by NATO-aligned countries can lead to very high energy prices, as experienced recently in Europe and Asia. As a result, fossil fuel companies find themselves being pushed to increase oil and gas and even coal production. This is motivated both by the desire of governments to minimize the burden on their constituents, but also by companies and its investors seeking to take advantage of the windfall profits that are flowing. The crucial question is whether this ‘off-ramp’ from their decarbonization pathway is temporary and reversible, or whether it might be adopted strategically to extract longer-term relaxation of efforts to decarbonize.
## Methods
We sourced the equity production data of global coal, oil and natural gas producers in the year 2010–2018 from the Carbon Major Project hosted by the Climate Accountability Institute at <https://climateaccountability.org/carbonmajors.html>. In addition, we sourced coal production data of the top 50 Chinese coal producers in the year 2010–2018 (ranking by production rate in 2017) from the China Coal Industry Yearbook and companies’ public disclosures. The production data for fossil fuel have been converted to the unit exajoule (EJ) to be consistent with fossil fuel demand projections from the IEA and the Integrated Assessment Models (IAMs). The conversion factors were sourced from _BP’s Statistical Review of World Energy 2021_ (ref. [23](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR23 "Statistical Review of World Energy 70th edn \(BP, 2021\); 
                  https://www.bp.com/content/dam/bp/business-sites/en/global/corporate/pdfs/energy-economics/statistical-review/bp-stats-review-2021-full-report.pdf
                  
                ")) and are provided in the sheets ‘conversion coal’, ‘conversion oil’ and ‘conversion gas’ of the [Source Data](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#Sec11). Coal, oil and gas were respectively converted from million tonnes, million barrels and billion cubic feet to exajoules using these conversion factors.
The datasets combined cover 74 coal companies representing 56% of the global coal production, 67 oil companies representing 76% of the global oil production and 70 natural gas companies representing 76% of the global natural gas production during 2010–2020. There were 142 companies in total, with several of them producing more than one type of fossil fuel. The world’s annual production data were sourced from _BP’s Statistical Review of World Energy 2021_ (ref. [23](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR23 "Statistical Review of World Energy 70th edn \(BP, 2021\); 
                  https://www.bp.com/content/dam/bp/business-sites/en/global/corporate/pdfs/energy-economics/statistical-review/bp-stats-review-2021-full-report.pdf
                  
                ")). We distinguished between IOCs and SOEs, where companies are classified as state-owned if more than 50% of the company is owned by a government[17](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR17 "Rekker, S., O’Brien, K., Humphrey, J. & Pascale, A. Comparing extraction rates of fossil fuel producers against global climate goals. Nat. Clim. Change 8, 489–492 \(2018\).").
Fossil fuel demand trajectories were extracted from IAM scenarios from the SSP database hosted by the IIASA Energy Program at <https://tntcat.iiasa.ac.at/SspDb>. There are 13 SSP–RCP1.9 scenarios available from the six IAM frameworks including AIM/CGE, GCAM4, IMAGE, MESSAGE-GLOBIOM, REMIND-MAGPIE and WITCH-GLOBIOM, which represent the primary IPCC 1.5 °C trajectories. It has been suggested that “across all 13 available scenarios, net-zero GHG emissions are reached around 2055–2075 (rounded to the nearest 5 years)” and these scenarios will limit end-of-century radiative forcing to 1.9 W m−2 scenarios, and consequently restrict median warming in the year 2100 to below 1.5 °C (ref. [29](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR29 "Rogelj, J. et al. Scenarios towards limiting global mean temperature increase below 1.5 °C. Nat. Clim. Change 8, 325–332 \(2018\).")). Consequently, “all scenarios keep warming to below 2 °C with a more than 66% probability, and maximum (peak) median temperature estimates vary from 1.5 °C to 1.8 °C” (ref. [29](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR29 "Rogelj, J. et al. Scenarios towards limiting global mean temperature increase below 1.5 °C. Nat. Clim. Change 8, 325–332 \(2018\).")), which is consistent with 1.5 °C of warming above pre-industrial levels with a ‘low’ overshoot. The IEA has also recently published the Net Zero Emissions Roadmap with a fossil fuel demand trajectory which was also adopted in this study[30](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR30 "Net Zero by 2050: A Roadmap for the Global Energy Sector \(International Energy Agency, 2021\).").
The five SSPs examine how changes in global societal behaviour, demographics and economics over the next century could impact global emissions and are used extensively in the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report. The SSPs are based on five narratives: a world of sustainability-focused growth and equality (SSP1); a ‘middle of the road’ world where trends broadly follow their historical patterns (SSP2); a fragmented world of ‘resurgent nationalism’ (SSP3); a world of ever-increasing inequality (SSP4); and a world of rapid and unconstrained growth in economic output and energy use (SSP5). An RCP is a greenhouse gas concentration (not emissions) trajectory that was adopted by the IPCC before the development of the SSPs. The RCPs are labelled after a possible range of radiative forcing values in the year 2100 (for example, RCP2.6 = 2.6 W m−2 and produces average global temperature anomalies of around 2 degrees above pre-industrial levels by 2100). Since the IPCC Fifth Assessment Report, the original pathways have been combined with SSPs. The SSPs each produce different RCPs given the level of carbon taxation applied to the global economy (for example, SSP1–RCP2.6 and SSP5–RCP8.5). Along with these new scenario–pathway combinations, new RCPs have also been introduced (including 1.9), which are the RCPs consistent with 1.5 degrees above pre-industrial levels.
For composing 1.5 °C Paris-aligned production trajectories for individual fossil fuel producers, we followed ref. [9](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR9 "Rohleder, M., Wilkens, M. & Zink, J. The effects of mutual fund decarbonisation on stock prices and carbon emissions. J. Bank. Financ. 134, 106352 \(2022\).") to identify Paris-compliant pathways. This approach first required the selected decarbonization pathways to be consistent with WB2D or in our case 1.5 °C pathways (since we focused on 1.5 °C-consistent pathways). Second, it required the base year from which progress is measured to be consistent with the starting year of the underlying base year (for an explanation, see ref. [14](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR14 "Rekker, S., Ives, M. C., Wade, B., Webb, L. & Greig, C. Measuring corporate Paris Compliance using a strict science-based approach. Nat. Commun. 13, 4441 \(2022\).")).
### Eligible decarbonization pathways and base year used
To identify eligible pathways, we selected IPCC IAM pathways that are consistent with 1.5 °C and started in 2015 or earlier. We identified six pathways that commence in 2010 and seven that commence in 2005. For demonstration purposes, we chose to use three 1.9 scenarios/pathways (that is, AIM/CGE SSP1, MESSAGE-GLOBIOM SSP2, GCAM4 SSP5). These three scenarios were chosen because they are publicly available in the IIASA database, were developed by modelling groups recognized by the IPCC, represent illustrative 1.5-degree pathway archetypes from three different SSPs[31](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR31 "IPCC Special Report on Global Warming of 1.5 °C \(eds Masson-Delmotte, V. et al.\) \(WMO, 2018\).") and are aligned with our ‘Paris compliance’ requirement that the decarbonization pathway begins on or before 2015. The scenarios we used have historical data from 2010 to 2014 and model from 2015 onwards[29](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR29 "Rogelj, J. et al. Scenarios towards limiting global mean temperature increase below 1.5 °C. Nat. Clim. Change 8, 325–332 \(2018\)."). Therefore, the year 2014 was selected as the base year. We did not make any amendments to these scenarios to account for a potential mismatch between the global production in the base year and that used in the IPCC IAM pathways. As stated in the Supplementary Materials of ref. [29](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR29 "Rogelj, J. et al. Scenarios towards limiting global mean temperature increase below 1.5 °C. Nat. Clim. Change 8, 325–332 \(2018\)."), the 2010 emissions for each of these scenarios fall within the uncertainty range of estimate historical global CO2 emissions in that year[29](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR29 "Rogelj, J. et al. Scenarios towards limiting global mean temperature increase below 1.5 °C. Nat. Clim. Change 8, 325–332 \(2018\)."). Note that we could apply our methodology to any other IPCC or global decarbonization pathways that apply different allocation methods, provided they also complied with our ‘Paris compliance’ requirements. Using earlier base years and other allocation methods would require more data but is an option that is certainly worth exploring.
For comparison purposes, we also used the IEA’s NZE roadmap demand projections, which starts from 2020. This pathway uses real-world performance tracing back to 2010 as it takes the global fossil fuel companies’ historical performance into account. Thus, if the global fossil fuel companies follow IEA’s NZE roadmap from 2020 onwards, 1.5 °C limits would be kept. In terms of cumulative production, global fossil fuel companies might follow any of the three SSP–RCP1.9 scenarios from the base year 2014 or IEA’s NZE roadmap from the base year 2020 to be on track with 1.5 °C trajectories, but with the proviso that they can verify that CCS requirements have been and will continue to be met under the trajectory. We indicated the riskiness of the different pathways, given their high reliance on CCS, by including ‘flags’: a ‘purple’ flag for SSP5, a ‘yellow’ flag for SSP2 and IEA-NZE and a ‘brown’ flag for SSP1. Using the IEA-NZE pathway ignores the inequality of budget allocation by shifting the base year, allowing companies to ignore their production before 2020 (ref. [14](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR14 "Rekker, S., Ives, M. C., Wade, B., Webb, L. & Greig, C. Measuring corporate Paris Compliance using a strict science-based approach. Nat. Commun. 13, 4441 \(2022\).")). Note that all the scenarios below rely on different assumptions on societal and economic developments, and on extensive use of CCS and carbon removal technologies. We demonstrate these differences in Table [1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#Tab1) for the scenarios we used. Finally, if a company’s production is not currently aligned with a pathway, it does not necessarily mean that the company cannot re-align itself; a company’s commitments to future production and CCS levels may make its plans aligned with a cumulative 1.5 °C production budget and CCS requirements.
### Allocation of production budget using 2010–2014 production
There are many ways to allocate a burnable fossil fuel production budget. For demonstration purposes, we allocated the annual production budget on the basis of a company’s share of the world’s total production from 2010 to 2014. A number of studies have concluded that any new investment in fossil fuel-based assets will be inconsistent with a 1.5-degree scenario without stranding assets[29](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR29 "Rogelj, J. et al. Scenarios towards limiting global mean temperature increase below 1.5 °C. Nat. Clim. Change 8, 325–332 \(2018\)."),[32](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR32 "Tong, D. et al. Committed emissions from existing energy infrastructure jeopardize 1.5 °C climate target. Nat. Clim. Change 572, 373–377 \(2019\)."),[33](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR33 "Pfeiffer, A., Hepburn, C., Vogt-Schilb, A. & Caldecott, B. Committed emissions from existing and planned power plants and asset stranding required to meet the Paris Agreement. Environ. Res. Lett. 13, 054019 \(2018\)."), which is consistent with the 2014 base year of the SSP–RCP1.9 scenarios that we used. Note that if a decarbonization scenario starting in 2005 is applied, the allocation would be the average share of production between 2005 and the year to which historical production is used, making the company accountable since the year production is projected from.
The equation is given as:
$${{\rm{CPB}}}_{{i},\,{j},{k}}\,={{\rm{GPB}}}_{{j},{k},{e}}\times {{\rm{RCP}}}_{{i},\,{j},{k}}\,$$
(1) 
$${{\rm{RCP}}}_{{i},\,{j},{k}}=\,\frac{\mathop{\sum }\nolimits_{k=2010}^{k=2014}{{\rm{CP}}}_{i,\,j,k}}{\mathop{\sum }\nolimits_{k=2010}^{k=2014}{{\rm{TCP}}}_{j,k}}\,$$
(2) 
where \\({{\rm{CPB}}}_{{i},\,{j},{k}}\\) represents the annual company production budget (CPB) in the year _k_ of company _i_ for fuel _j_ (in EJ). GPB _j_ ,_k_ ,_e_ represents the global production budget (GPB) for fuel _j_ in the year _k_ of the chosen decarbonization pathway _e_ (in EJ). \\({{\rm{RCP}}}_{{i},\,{j},{k}}\\) represents the ratio of company _i_ ’s production to the world’s annual production during the period 2010–2014. \\(\,{\mathop{\sum }\limits_{k=2010}^{k=2014}}{{\rm{CP}}_{i,\,j,k}}\\) is the sum of company _i_ ’s production of fuel _j_ from the year 2010 to 2014 (in EJ) while \\({\mathop{\sum }\limits_{k=2010}^{k=2014}}{{\rm{TCP}}_{j,k}}\\) is the sum of the world’s total annual production from the year 2010 to 2014 (in EJ). The world’s annual production data were sourced from ref. [22](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0#ref-CR22 "Heede, R. & Oreskes, N. Potential emissions of CO2 and methane from proved reserves of fossil fuels: an alternative analysis. Glob. Environ. Change 36, 12–20 \(2016\).").
Even if production exceeds the global production budget under a certain scenario, it does not necessarily mean that the 1.5 °C carbon budget would be exceeded since the carbon budget is directly related to energy consumption rather than production. However, aligning the production trajectories with fossil fuel demands gives an optimal benchmark for companies and their stakeholders to maximize profit without oversupplies over time in a carbon-constrained world.
### Remaining production budget (RPB)
We deduct the company’s cumulative production since 2014 from the company’s cumulative company production budget from 2014 to 2050. Thus,
$${{\rm{RPB}}}_{{i},\,{j},{k},{e}}\,={{\rm{TCPB}}}_{{j},{e}}-\sum _{k}{{\rm{CP}}}_{{i},\,{j},{k}}$$
(3) 
$${\rm{TCP}{B}}_{j,e}=\mathop{\sum }\limits_{k=2014}^{2050}{{\rm{CPB}}}_{{i},\,{j},{k}}$$
